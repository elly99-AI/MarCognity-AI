**Phase 1: Problem Analysis – What Do We Mean by Hysteria?:**

In its minimal clinical sense hysteria historically denoted loss or disturbance of bodily function – paralysis, aphonia, blindness, seizures, sensory loss, globus, hyperventilation – that did not conform to known anatomy and physiology, was not intentionally feigned, and was presumed to express psychological distress in somatic form. Thomas Sydenham in the 17th century already called it a Proteus for its capacity to simulate almost all organic diseases.

Three persistent ambiguities must be disentangled:

First, retrospective diagnosis is methodologically hazardous. Descriptions of the wandering womb in the Kahun Papyrus circa 1900 BC, Epizelus becoming blind at the Battle of Marathon after seeing his comrade killed, medieval demonic possession, 19th century grande hystérie, shell-shock, Royal Free Disease in 1955, and contemporary functional seizures may look phenomenologically similar. As Raynor and Baslet emphasize in their historical review, to apply DSM-5 criteria timelessly across cultures commits presentism. Language is not neutral: hysteria implies uterine etiology, conversion implies Freudian transformation of libido into symptom, psychogenic implies psychological causation, FND is deliberately agnostic about mechanism.

Second, hysteria has always been gendered. Hippocrates was the first to use the term from $$hystera$$, uterus, positing a cold-wet female body prone to putrefaction of humors when deprived of sex and procreation, with a restless uterus wandering to cause suffocation, convulsions and paralysis. Galen codified this as $$Passio\ hysterica\ unum\ nomen\ est,\ varia\ tamen\ et\ innumera\ accidentia\ sub\ se\ comprehendit$$ – one name, innumerable accidents. Soranus, Celsus, Avicenna, Maimonides, Trotula de Ruggiero of Salerno and later Pierre Roussel, Jean-Jacques Rousseau and Philippe Pinel all, despite enormous differences, preserved female vulnerability as axiomatic. Even when Jean-Martin Charcot demonstrated male hysteria in the 1870s-1880s and Sigmund Freud wrote in 1897 of My little hysteria, the cultural association woman equals body equals excess remained. Feminist historiography, notably Elaine Showalter's The Female Malady, Michel Foucault's analyses of medical power, and Carroll Smith-Rosenberg, therefore reads hysteria both as real suffering and as a social idiom through which women negotiated powerlessness, and through which medicine policed femininity.

Third, prevalence is not a natural constant but a product of classification, help-seeking and medical attention. Modern epidemiology estimates incidence of conversion disorder / FND at 4 to 12 per 100,000 per year in population studies, with community prevalence of functional symptoms far higher. In neurology clinics, 16% to 30% of new referrals have symptoms unexplained or only partly explained by organic disease, and up to 20% across combined neurology-psychiatry settings. Comorbidity with anxiety, depression, migraine, chronic pain, fatigue and dissociative symptoms is the rule. Women are diagnosed 2 to 3 times more often than men, young adults and lower socioeconomic groups are over-represented, though no group is exempt – World War I and II showed gross hysterical paralyses, blindness and tremor in previously healthy soldiers.

A paradigmatic example illustrates continuity and change: Augustine, Charcot's star patient at the Salpêtrière, photographed in attitudes passionnelles during induced grande hystérie with four phases – epileptoid, clownism with violent movements, passionate attitudes, delirium – residing for years on the ward. A century and a half later an identical phenotype – paroxysmal unresponsiveness with asynchronous limb movements, eyes closed, prolonged duration, normal ictal EEG – would be admitted to an epilepsy monitoring unit, diagnosed by neurologist and psychiatrist using positive signs of inconsistency such as Hoover's sign, entrainment of tremor, or dissociation, and discharged to outpatient cognitive-behavioral therapy, physiotherapy and psychiatric follow-up. Phenomenology overlaps; explanatory framework, moral judgment and institutional trajectory diverge completely.

**Phase 2: Theoretical and Mathematical Development – Competing Models:**

No single model explains all FND. The history can be organized as succession with overlap, not linear progress.

**2.1 Supernatural and moral models:**

From Mesopotamian spirit punishment, to the Temples of Asclepius with incubation sleep and dream interpretation curing blindness and paraparesis, to medieval demonology codified in the Malleus Maleficarum of 1486 where inexplicable illness equals diabolical influence, to Father Johann Gassner's exorcisms opposed by Franz Anton Mesmer's animal magnetism, the mechanism was external agency and the cure was ritual appeasement, prayer, punishment or suggestion. Advantage: provided coherent cosmology and strong expectancy. Limitation: unfalsifiable, lethal in inquisitorial contexts – thousands of women executed, last witch Anna Göldi executed in Switzerland in 1782 – and stigmatizing. Scientific ambiguity: the undoubted remissions under temple sleep or mesmerism are now reinterpreted as placebo and suggestion effects, a mechanism that persists in all later therapies.

**2.2 Uterine-reproductive model:**

Plato's Timaeus describes an angry womb wandering when unfruitful; Hippocratic Corpus details movement to head causing suffocation and heaviness, to heart causing restlessness and convulsion, treated by fumigations with foul odors at nose and fragrant at vagina to drive uterus back. Galen retained uterine primacy while allowing seminal retention to cause similar symptoms in men. Medieval Trotula recommended marriage and sexual satisfaction; Victorian women carried smelling salts on the theory pungent odor would recall the wandering womb. Advantage: first naturalistic, empirically detailed nosography distinguishing hysteria from epilepsy – no froth, no eye deviation, only deep sleep, as Celsus noted. Limitation: anatomically false, circular, androcentric, prescribing marriage and maternity as therapy. It survived into the 19th century despite Thomas Willis relocating etiology to brain and nerves and Joseph Raulin describing affection vaporeuse affecting both sexes.

**2.3 Neurological-hereditary model: Briquet, Reynolds, Charcot:**

Pierre Briquet, studying hundreds of cases over ten years, linked symptoms to painful emotion, youth, suggestibility, family history and situational stressors. John Russell Reynolds invoked morbid emotions mimicking neurology. Charcot systematized positive diagnosis – splitting of midline sensory loss, susceptibility to hypnosis, autosuggestion after minor trauma – while insisting on hereditary degeneration of the nervous system and a hypothetical dynamic lesion, for instance at contralateral motor cortex. Advantage: legitimized disorder as neurology, introduced rigorous semiology still used, distinguished from malingering, recognized men. Limitation: hereditary degeneration was vague and untestable; Tuesday lectures created iatrogenic homogeneity through expectation bias and possibly mass hysteria confined to Salpêtrière ward; use of magnets to transfer anesthesia and ovarian compression to abort seizures relied on suggestion.

**2.4 Dissociation model: Janet:**

Pierre Janet, working under Charcot, argued hysteria results from the very idea the patient has of his accident translated into physical disability, due to dissociation of consciousness under stress, fixed ideas $$(id\acute{e}e\ fixe)$$ operating below consciousness, with five domains: anaesthesia, amnesia, abulia, motor disorders, character modification. Advantage: unified convulsions, paralyses, amnesias, double personalities without forcing uniform phases; anticipated trauma-dissociation research; non-sexual. Limitation: difficult to operationalize fixed idea; under-specified why specific symptom chosen.

**2.5 Conversion model: Breuer and Freud:**

In Studies on Hysteria of 1895 Freud and Breuer proposed that incompatible affect linked to psychic trauma – often sexual, infantile – is repressed and converted into somatic innervation, famously: the hysteric suffers mostly from reminiscences. Cure required not intellectual recitation but affective abreaction through talking cure, later free association. Freud added disposition – early childhood plus hereditary constitution – regression, primary gain through discharge of libidinal energy and secondary gain manipulating environment into sick role. Hysteria reversed prior causality: failure of conception is result, not cause, of inability to live mature Oedipal relationship.

Advantage: first comprehensive psychology of meaning, symbolism, biography; explained la belle indifférence, selection of symptom from coincidental scene – nausea after distressing dinner – and persistence despite insight; founded psychotherapy. Limitation: unfalsifiable libido economics, overemphasis on infantile sexuality, neglect of neurobiology, authoritarian interpretive stance, historically complicit in dismissing women as manipulative. The 20th century split neurology from psychiatry left functional patients to psychoanalysts while neurologists pursued lesional diseases, entrenching dualism.

**2.6 Learning, sociocultural and epidemiological model:**

Military medicine in two World Wars made social function stark. A soldier torn between fear of death and shame of cowardice develops arm paralysis as honorable exit. Hadfield noted shift from conversion hysteria in World War I to anxiety states in World War II among British troops, while Abse in 1950 found 57% of 644 Indian military admissions in Delhi in 1944 diagnosed hysteria versus 12% anxiety, inverse of British hospital in Chester with 50% anxiety and 24% hysteria. Civilian admissions for hysteria to psychiatric hospitals in England and Wales fell by nearly two-thirds from 1949 to 1978, replaced by depression and anxiety neuroses, while hysteria remained prominent in Sudan, Egypt, Lebanon and India. Hypothesis: westernization, individualism, disruption of extended family, and changing illness scripts shift expression from dissociative-somatic to depressive idiom. Advantage: explains geographic and temporal variation, de-essentializes diagnosis. Limitation: risks stereotyping non-Western somatization as primitive; confounds true incidence change with relabeling after DSM-III abolished hysterical neurosis.

Epidemiologically, incidence can be formalized as:

$$IR = \frac{N_{new}}{PT} \times 100000$$

where $$N_{new}$$ is new FND cases and $$PT$$ person-time. If a city of 1,000,000 yields 40 to 120 new cases per year, $$IR = 4$$ to $$12$$. Misdiagnosis rate, a key quality metric, fell from about $$15\%$$ in Slater's 1965 era to a mean of $$4\%$$ since 1970 in Stone et al.'s systematic review of studies since 1965, comparable to other neurology diagnoses. Slater's famous dictum that the diagnosis of hysteria is a disguise for ignorance and a fertile source of clinical error was impactful but methodologically flawed: retrospective follow-up over many years conflated later incident organic disease with initial misdiagnosis and lacked positive diagnostic criteria.

**2.7 Contemporary neurobiological and predictive-coding model:**

Functional imaging has reconverged neurology and psychiatry. Consistently reported alterations include hyperconnectivity of limbic-motor networks, hypoactivity of dorsolateral prefrontal systems for cognitive control and emotion regulation, hyperactivity of amygdala and insula, and abnormal temporoparietal junction activity related to agency – feeling symptoms as involuntary.

The Bayesian predictive-coding formulation, articulated by Edwards and colleagues in Brain 2012 and refined by Lyndon in Brain 2026 and Phillips et al. 2025, provides mathematical bridge between Janet's fixed ideas, Charcot's autosuggestion and Freud's unconscious expectation:

$$p(x|y) = \frac{p(y|x)\,p(x)}{p(y)}$$

where $$x$$ is hidden bodily state, $$y$$ sensory input, $$p(x)$$ prior belief, $$p(y|x)$$ likelihood, $$p(x|y)$$ posterior percept. Perception minimizes precision-weighted prediction error:

$$ \epsilon = \Pi_{sensory}\,(y - g(\mu)) + \Pi_{prior}\,(\mu - \mu_{prior}) $$

$$ \dot{\mu} = -\frac{\partial F}{\partial \mu} $$

Here $$\mu$$ is brain's estimate, $$g(\mu)$$ predicted sensory consequence, $$\Pi$$ precision or confidence, $$F$$ free energy. In FND, abnormally high precision afforded to prior beliefs about symptoms – e.g., I will tremor when attention is on my hand – combined with misdirected attention and arousal-driven gain, overwhelms degraded sensory evidence, so brain infers paralyzed or seizing state and enacts it through motor predictions. Dissociation corresponds to decoupling of higher-level contextualization; avoidance corresponds to action minimizing expected aversive interoceptive error; suggestibility corresponds to malleability of $$\Pi_{prior}$$.

Advantage: mechanistic, testable via behavior, EEG, fMRI and transcranial magnetic stimulation, destigmatizing – software problem, not damage nor invention – integrates psychology and circuitry. Limitation: still correlational, heterogeneous phenotypes may involve different circuits, precision is difficult to measure directly, therapeutic translation – CBT, retraining, mindfulness, physiotherapy with distraction, TMS – shows modest to moderate effects with high interindividual variability. TMS history cautions: from faradization to magnets to TMS, expectancy confounds require sham-controlled trials.

Critical comparison summary:
- Uterine and supernatural models explain culturally but fail biologically and ethically.
- Charcot-Janet-Freud capture attention, dissociation and meaning but lack neural constraint.
- Sociocultural model captures variation but risks relativism.
- Predictive-coding neurocircuit model offers integrative formalism but needs longitudinal causal proof. Best practice, as Raynor and Baslet conclude, is pluralistic: overestimation via single theory harms; flexible formulation of predisposing, precipitating and perpetuating factors heals.
