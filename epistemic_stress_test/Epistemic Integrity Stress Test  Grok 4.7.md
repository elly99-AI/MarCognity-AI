## Phase 1. Problem Analysis

The protection of sensitive data and the protection of intellectual property (IP) constitute neither the same legal issue nor the same economic problem, although they converge within contemporary information systems. Confusing the two leads to both inefficient overprotection and avoidable data leaks.

**Sensitive data (or special categories of personal data).**
 Under Regulation (EU) 2016/679 (GDPR), personal data is defined as any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person (Art. 4.1). The "special categories" listed in Article 9—such as racial or ethnic origin, political opinions, religious beliefs, genetic data, biometric data used for unique identification, and data concerning health, sex life, or sexual orientation—require a heightened legal basis for processing. The legal interest being protected is the individual's informational autonomy, not the competitive advantage of the organization holding the data. Mondschein and Monda (2018) characterize the GDPR as a regulatory mechanism combining principles of lawfulness, purpose limitation, data minimization, accuracy, storage limitation, integrity, confidentiality, and accountability. However, Ducato (2020) demonstrates that GDPR information obligations conflict with scientific research: granular informed consent may be incompatible with legitimate secondary uses of research data, meaning that Article 89 (safeguards for archiving, research, and historical purposes) serves as an imperfect safety valve. **Intellectual property and trade secrets.** Traditional IP (patents, copyright, trademarks, designs) grants temporary exclusive rights in exchange—in the case of patents—for disclosure. Trade secrecy is structurally different. Article 39 of the TRIPS Agreement (1994) requires WTO members to protect undisclosed information that is secret, has commercial value because it is secret, and has been subject to reasonable steps to keep it secret. Directive (EU) 2016/943 harmonizes this definition within the Union and defines unlawful acquisition, use, or disclosure. In the United States, the Defend Trade Secrets Act of 2016 (DTSA) creates a federal civil cause of action for misappropriation, featuring *ex parte* injunctive relief in limited circumstances and immunity for whistleblowers. The Searle report for the UK Intellectual Property Office (Searle, n.d., published by GOV.UK) summarizes the empirical consensus: for many innovative firms, secrecy is the preferred appropriation mechanism, rather than a mere fallback for what cannot be patented.

Three structural tensions define the current problem:

1. **Conflict of objectives.** The holder of a trade secret desires permanent opacity. The GDPR data subject seeks access, rectification, erasure, and an explanation of automated decisions. CJEU case law has clarified that invoking trade secrecy is not sufficient, in itself, to deny a data subject access to the logic behind an automated decision; nor does it mandate disclosure of the entire model. The balance to be struck is procedural, not substantive.
2. **Hybrid assets.** A generative model's training dataset may simultaneously contain personal data, copyright-protected works, third-party trade secrets, and proprietary know-how. Buick (2025) documents that many AI companies fail to maintain adequate internal records regarding the provenance of training data; this hinders both the ability to assert a "fair use" or text-and-data mining exception defense and compliance with GDPR data retention periods. A developer who retains data to defend against a copyright claim may violate privacy regulations regarding retention limits, whereas one who deletes the data may be left defenseless in IP litigation. This tension—defensive retention versus data minimization—represents one of the most acute regulatory challenges today.
3. **Cyber ​​threats as a common vector.** Both personal data and trade secrets are exfiltrated via the same channels: insiders, cloud misconfigurations, compromised APIs, and—increasingly—AI-enabled attacks. The IBM and Ponemon Institute report on the cost of a data breach (2026), based on 602 organizations that experienced incidents between March 2025 and February 2026, places the global average cost at $4.99 million; AI-enabled malicious breaches—accounting for one in four incidents, a 56% year-over-year increase—cost an average of $6 million. More than 20% of organizations reported a breach targeting AI models or applications, primarily caused by compromised APIs, plug-ins, or surrounding applications (27%) and by cloud misconfigurations affecting AI workloads (27%). Regarding ransomware, intellectual property serves as leverage in 31% of reported cases, ranking behind brand reputation (41%) and employee data (35%).

Wang (2025) provides econometric evidence that intellectual property protection is negatively associated with corporate data breach risk, and that this mechanism operates in part through heightened organizational awareness of data protection. Establishing causality is not straightforward, as jurisdictions with stronger IP protection may also be home to companies with more mature cybersecurity practices. Nevertheless, the finding aligns with the thesis that appropriation and confidentiality regimes reinforce each other when an organization internalizes both as aspects of intangible asset management rather than as isolated compliance requirements.

## Phase 2. Theoretical and mathematical development

### 2.1. The incomplete IP social contract and the anomalous case of secrecy

The standard economic justification for IP is an exchange: static inefficiency (temporary exclusivity) in return for dynamic efficiency (incentives to innovate plus disclosure). Patents and copyright fit this model reasonably well. Trade secrecy fits only partially; there is no disclosure and no predetermined expiration. Hettinger (1989), cited in Searle’s review, formulates the classic critique: secrecy restricts the flow of ideas, labor mobility, and competition. The institutional counter-argument is that such protection is weaker than patent protection: it does not cover independent discovery or lawful reverse engineering (Directive 2016/943, recitals and articles; DTSA, which preserves these defenses).

Searle cites Passman’s (2014) sectoral estimate: the misappropriation of trade secrets is estimated to cost between 1% and 3% of GDP in developed economies. This figure is an industry estimate rather than an accounting measurement and should be treated as an order of magnitude subject to selection bias (only reported cases are observed). Nevertheless, data on usage are more robust. Hall, Helmers, Sena, and Rogers (2012), cited by Searle, find that approximately 70% of British firms engaging in product and process innovation use secrets to protect those innovations; usage is higher among large firms (59%) than small ones (28%) and higher among firms that patent (82%) than those that do not. Cohen, Nelson, and Walsh (2000) had previously shown, in the context of US manufacturing, that secrecy and lead time were preferred over patents for both product and process innovations. The conventional wisdom that treated the patent as the “gold standard” is empirically outdated.

Anton and Yao (2004) model the choice between patenting and secrecy based on the size of the innovation: when formal protection is weak, major innovations tend to be kept secret while minor ones are patented, because disclosing a major innovation via a patent facilitates imitation in areas not covered by the claims. Using a natural experiment involving the uneven strengthening of state trade secret laws in the U.S., Png (2017) documents a nearly 40% drop in patents the following year in states that strengthened secrecy protections, suggesting substitutability rather than mere complementarity. The policy implication is uncomfortable: strengthening secrecy can reduce patent-based disclosure and, consequently, knowledge spillovers.

### 2.2. Differential privacy as a formal bound, not a metaphor

Differential privacy (Dwork, McSherry, Nissim, and Smith, 2006; algorithmic foundations in Dwork and Roth, 2014, with over 14,000 citations in subsequent literature) offers the only mathematically operational definition of "protection of the individual against data analysis" that withstands an adversary's auxiliary knowledge.

A randomized mechanism \(M\) is \(\varepsilon\)-differentially private if, for every pair of adjacent databases \(x, x'\) (differing by a single individual's record) and for every measurable set \(S\) of possible outputs,

\[
\Pr[M(x) \in S] \le e^{\varepsilon} \Pr[M(x') \in S].
\]

The \((\varepsilon, \delta)\)-DP relaxation allows for a probability \(\delta\) of catastrophic failure:

\[
\Pr[M(x) \in S] \le e^{\varepsilon} \Pr[M(x') \in S] + \delta.
\]

\(\varepsilon\) is not an intuitive "privacy score." It is a multiplier of the likelihood ratio. With \(\varepsilon = 0.1\), \(e^{\varepsilon} \approx 1.105\): the presence or absence of an individual barely alters the output distribution. With \(\varepsilon = 10\), \(e^{\varepsilon} \approx 22,026\): the formal guarantee exists, but it is weak for almost any realistic adversarial use case. Dwork (2019) highlights that the choice of \(\varepsilon\) entails two distinct uncertainties: that of social optimality (how much utility we are willing to sacrifice) and that of interpretation (what a large \(\varepsilon\) accumulated over many queries means operationally).

The Laplace mechanism for a query with sensitivity \(\Delta f\) (the maximum change in \(f\) upon altering a single record) adds noise drawn from \(\mathrm{Lap}(\Delta f / \varepsilon)\). Basic composition is linear: \(k\) mechanisms providing \(\varepsilon_i\)-DP compose to yield \(\sum \varepsilon_i\)-DP. Advanced composition and concentrated differential privacy (Dwork and Rothblum, 2016, arXiv:1603.01887) improve privacy accounting but do not eliminate the finite budget constraint. Cummings et al. (2024) review the state of the art and observe that the gap between theory and deployment—specifically the choice of \(\varepsilon\), accounting within production systems, and communicating risk to non-experts—remains the bottleneck, rather than the availability of mechanisms.

**Critical limitation regarding IP.** Differential privacy protects the *individual* within a dataset. It does not protect trade secrets embedded in model weights, an author's work against model training, or the know-how behind a chemical process. A model can be \(\varepsilon\)-DP with respect to its training data yet still memorize fragments of works if the adjacency neighborhood is not defined at the document level, or leak a secret if that secret is correlated across many records (the adversary does not need a specific person's record; they need the pattern). Treating DP as a universal solution for "sensitive data + IP" is a category error.

Unresolved ambiguities:

- **Substitutability vs. complementarity.** Png (2017) suggests a short-term substitutability between patents and trade secrets. Arora, Athreye, and Huang (2013), using British data from the Survey on Innovation and Patent Use, find that patenting firms protect a *larger* proportion of their most valuable innovations via secrecy. Both observations may hold true at different scales: substitution at the invention level, and complementarity at the firm-portfolio level.
- **Labor mobility.** Png and Samila (2013) associate rulings contrary to the U.S. "inevitable disclosure" doctrine with approximately 10% higher mobility among college-educated workers. Contigiani, Hsu, and Barankay (2018) link mobility restrictions to lower subsequent innovation. The trade secret holder gains appropriability in the short term, while the system loses out on spillovers in the medium term.
- **Cost of theft.** Carr and Gorman (2001) and subsequent studies document negative stock price reactions to announcements of trade secret theft. More recent literature on cybercrime (cited by Searle: Arcuri, Brogi, and Gandolfi, 2017; Hilary, Segal, and Zhang, 2016) finds small or ambiguous effects on firm performance. This discrepancy may stem from the market already discounting a baseline level of exfiltration, or from the fact that many losses go unannounced. Searle notes, citing PwC (2018), that in the case of cyber-theft of trade secrets, only about 10% of the cost is immediate; 90% represents a deferred loss of know-how, competitive advantage, and jobs. This cost structure means that "closed incident" metrics tend to underestimate the damage.


### 2.4. A minimal protection decision model

Let \(V\) be the private value of an intangible asset, \(p\) the probability of exfiltration if only basic controls are applied, \(c(s)\) the cost of a safeguard level \(s\) (encryption, DLP, compartmentalization, DP, insurance), and \(L\) the loss if the asset is compromised (including regulatory fines, reputational damage, and loss of advantage). The organization chooses \(s\) to minimize

\[
c(s) + p(s)\, L + \lambda \cdot D(s),
\]

where \(p(s)\) is decreasing and convex, \(D(s)\) is the loss of internal utility from restricting knowledge flows (the innovation cost documented by James, Leiblein, and Lu, 2013), and \(\lambda\) weights that cost. The interior optimum requires

\[
c'(s) + \lambda D'(s) = -p'(s)\, L.
\]

Three corollaries follow. First, if \(L\) is dominated by personal data fines (GDPR: up to 4% of annual global turnover or €20 million, whichever is higher), organizations overinvest in personal data controls and underinvest in process secrets where \(L\) is strategic but difficult to quantify to a board. Second, if \(D'(s)\) is high—as in R&D firms with a low-trust culture—strict secrecy can destroy more value than it protects; Hannah (2005) shows that a culture of trust, not just access control, predicts effective protection by employees. Third, generative AI shifts \(p(s)\) upward for a given \(s\): the IBM 2026 report indicates that attacks are launched for thousands of dollars while remediation continues to cost millions, and that organizations using AI and automation in security operations reduce the average cost of a breach by nearly $2 million. Technology represents both the adverse shift in \(p(s)\) and the technology that can lower \(c(s)\).

Protecting one does not automatically protect the other. Encryption, access restrictions, and avoiding data retention "just in case" resolve more real-world incidents than any elegant contractual clause.

## Phase 5. Synthesis, applications, and review of reference literature

**Points that withstand scrutiny.**

1. Trade secrets and personal data constitute distinct legal regimes. The former protects competitive advantage; the latter, individual autonomy. Their coexistence within the same systems is contingent, not conceptual.
2. Secrecy is the dominant appropriation mechanism for many process innovations and—in the UK—for approximately 70% of firms innovating in both products and processes (Hall et al., 2012, via Searle). Firms that patent do not abandon secrecy; they use it more.
3. Macroeconomic evidence regarding theft (1–3% of GDP; Passman, 2014) is a weak industry estimate. Micro-level evidence on appropriation preferences (Cohen et al., 2000; Arundel, 2001; Crass et al., 2019) is more robust, albeit based on self-reporting.
4. Differential privacy provides a formal bound regarding the individual, not the work or the secret itself. \(\varepsilon\) must be treated as an accounting budget, not a slogan.
5. In 2025–2026, the average cost of a data breach stands at $4.99 million; AI-enabled malicious breaches at $6 million; IP serves as leverage for ransomware in 31% of reported cases; and double encryption is present in only 37% of breached organizations (IBM/Ponemon, 2026).
6. Wang (2025) finds a negative association between IP protection and corporate breach risk, mediated in part by data protection awareness. This is a promising yet nascent result (7 citations at the time of the index check); it should not be elevated to a universal rule.

**Concrete example of the topic.** A pharmaceutical company holds three assets in the same data lake: (a) results from a trial involving health data (Art. 9 GDPR; basis under Art. 9.2, often scientific research with Art. 89 safeguards); (b) the manufacturing protocol for an active ingredient—left unpatented at certain stages because disclosure would facilitate process imitation (trade secret within the meaning of Art. 39 TRIPS and Directive 2016/943); and (c) manuals and diagrams that may constitute protected works. A misconfigured cloud provider—accounting for exactly 27% of AI workload breaches in the IBM 2026 report, cited here as a configuration analogy—could exfiltrate all three. A data protection fine does not remedy the loss of the process secret. Legal action under the DTSA or the Directive does not remedy the harm suffered by the data subject. Differential privacy applied to published trial statistics does not prevent the protocol—stored in plaintext—from leaking via an API. Effective protection lies at the intersection of asset classification, encryption at rest and in transit, data minimization, compartmentalization of process know-how from patient data, and an explicit litigation hold policy—not merely the disjoint sum of three PDF policies.

Omitted variable bias (governance quality, firm size, digital sector) is the usual suspect. Consistency with Searle’s thesis—that IP and cybersecurity reinforce each other when an organization treats intangibles as assets—is plausible but unproven. Seven early citations do not constitute a consensus. Improvement: difference-in-differences designs exploiting exogenous IP reforms, along the lines of Png (2017), rather than cross-sectional regressions.
- **Dwork and Roth (2014); Dwork and Rothblum (2016); Cummings et al. (2024).** Here, citation consistency is the field's gold standard. The \(\varepsilon\)-DP definition has been stable since 2006. The issue is not one of cross-citation but of translation: applied papers stating "we apply differential privacy" without specifying \(\varepsilon\), \(\delta\), the adjacency neighborhood, or composition accounting. Cummings et al. (2024) identify this as a practice gap. Any paper claiming IP protection via DP without those four specifications should be considered methodologically incomplete.
- **Buick (2025), *Journal of Intellectual Property Law & Practice*.** Proposes training transparency as a potential solution to the copyright-versus-data conflict. High, early citation counts (around 140 in the index consulted) indicate that the field is citing a diagnosis rather than an established theorem. A methodological weakness shared by almost all literature on AI and training is the opacity of the datasets: if companies do not record provenance (as noted by the *Washington Post* in April 2024, cited by Buick), neither regulators nor reviewers can verify claims regarding data minimization or mining exceptions. The solution: mandatory, third-party-auditable provenance records with limited retention periods—a design that simultaneously addresses copyright protection and the principle of time-bound data retention.

**Cross-cutting improvements that a serious article on this topic should incorporate.** State the protected interest on the first page (person, expression, secret, or hybrid). Do not use “sensitive data” as a synonym for “trade secret.” Report \(\varepsilon\) if Differential Privacy (DP) is invoked. Distinguish industrial estimation from causal identification. Cite Article 39 of the TRIPS Agreement, Directive 2016/943, and the DTSA based on their actual text, not law firm summaries. Update the cost of a data breach using the current IBM/Ponemon series and note the potential for sample bias. Treat the retention of training data as an explicit conflict between IP protection and data minimization, rather than relegating it to a footnote.

## Phase 6. Future implications, limitations, and research questions

**Applications that are already required, not futuristic.** Asset classification distinguishing between personal data, creative works, and trade secrets prior to the creation of any shared data lake. Encryption at rest and in transit as a condition for meeting the TRIPS "reasonable measure" standard, rather than a sign of advanced maturity; the 37% adoption rate observed in organizations that have already suffered breaches represents a failure to meet the baseline. Cryptographic inventory (only 34% visibility in the same sample) as a prerequisite for any post-quantum migration; the IBM 2026 report indicates that weaknesses in cryptographic management persist even as investment in quantum-resistant cryptography grows. SOC automation where the data justifies it: the nearly $2 million cost reduction is not merely a marketing talking point; it is the most directly actionable figure in the 2026 report. Differential privacy budgets for any statistics published based on personal data, including the disclosure of the accumulated \(\varepsilon\) value. Provenance logs for training data with explicit retention periods, avoiding the need to choose between copyright vulnerability and data minimization violations.

**Methodological limitations that will not disappear with more regulation.** Trade secrets are, by definition, difficult to observe. Surveys tend to underestimate figures. Litigation data overrepresents high-value secrets and disputes that were not settled privately. Natural experiments rely on staggered reforms that cannot be replicated at the researcher's convenience. Estimates placing the figure at 1–3% of GDP will not translate into national accounts data. Anyone promising to "measure IP theft" with two-decimal-place precision is selling a false sense of accuracy.

**Emerging directions, framed as questions rather than a normative agenda.**

1. Does the association identified by Wang (2025) hold up under a difference-in-differences design that exploits exogenous IP reforms, or is it merely a proxy for firm quality?
2. Which adjacency—whether person, document, or correlated process secret—renders a Differential Privacy (DP) guarantee relevant for hybrid assets, and at what level of \(\varepsilon\) does the AI ​​model's utility collapse?
3. Does the provenance transparency proposed by Buick (2025) reduce copyright litigation without increasing the attack surface regarding third-party secrets inadvertently included in the training set?
4. Does the shift from 4.99M to 6M in AI-enabled data breaches (IBM/Ponemon 2026) persist when controlling for critical infrastructure sectors (accounting for 62% of reported AI attacks; financial services at 6.3M, energy at 5.2M), or is it a result of sectoral composition effects?
5. Do the mobility constraints associated with trade secrecy (Png and Samila, 2013; Contigiani et al., 2018) have an analogue in the "no-training" and "no-retention" clauses found in 2024–2026 data contracts? Furthermore, what is their impact on process innovation among SMEs—firms that Searle identifies as being more vulnerable to cyber-theft and less capable of litigating?
6. When ransomware leverages IP in 31% of cases, is the firm's rational response to increase secrecy (less disclosure, fewer spillovers) or to rely more on patents (disclosure that diminishes blackmail value because the knowledge is already public)? The Anton and Yao (2004) model predicts the latter for incremental innovations and the former for major innovations. No one has estimated this using ransomware data from after 2023.

**Ethical scope of this discussion.** Nothing stated above constitutes an instruction to obtain, exfiltrate, bypass, or monetize personal data or the secrets of others. The scientific value lies in distinguishing between the legal interests at stake, quantifying published orders of magnitude, and highlighting where current practice—37% encryption rates, 34% cryptographic visibility, and unrecorded provenance—lags behind established doctrine. The protection of sensitive data and intellectual property is strengthened only when they are designed as distinct systems sharing a common technical layer, not when they are conflated as if they were one and the same.
