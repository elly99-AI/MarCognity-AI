## 1. Problem analysis: what is learned in conditioning?

Conditioning concerns how experience changes an organism’s responses to events. Its two principal forms—classical and operant conditioning—differ in the relations that are experimentally arranged:

- **Classical conditioning**, also called *Pavlovian conditioning*, concerns relationships between stimuli. A cue becomes informative about another event.
- **Operant conditioning**, also called *instrumental conditioning*, concerns relationships between actions and their consequences. A behavior changes because of what follows it.

This distinction is useful, but it is not an absolute division between “involuntary” and “voluntary” behavior. Both forms can involve expectation, attention, emotion, motivation, and physiological responses. In everyday environments, they usually operate together.

For example, the sound of a food-delivery notification may elicit anticipation because it predicts food: that is a plausible classical-conditioning process. Repeatedly opening the delivery app because doing so has previously produced a meal illustrates an operant relation. The notification may also increase the tendency to open the app, showing how a Pavlovian cue can influence instrumental action.

The central scientific question is not simply whether two events occur together. It is **which relations an organism learns, how that learning changes behavior, and under what conditions the learned response appears**.

## 2. Classical conditioning

### 2.1. Basic components

In a standard classical-conditioning procedure, an initially neutral cue is paired with an event that already elicits a response.

| Component | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Unconditioned stimulus (US) | An event that elicits a response without the training being studied | Food |
| Unconditioned response (UR) | Response elicited by the US | Salivation to food |
| Conditioned stimulus (CS) | A cue whose predictive significance is learned | A tone |
| Conditioned response (CR) | Response elicited by the CS after learning | Salivation to the tone |

The familiar sequence is:

\[
\text{Tone (CS)} \rightarrow \text{Food (US)}
\]

After repeated pairings, the tone alone can elicit a conditioned response. The CR need not be identical to the UR: it may be anticipatory, preparatory, or otherwise adapted to the relation between cue and event.

### 2.2. Contingency is more informative than mere pairing

A cue is especially informative when the US is more likely in its presence than in its absence. A common descriptive measure is:

\[
\Delta P = P(\text{US}\mid\text{CS})-P(\text{US}\mid\neg\text{CS}),
\]

where:

- \(P(\text{US}\mid\text{CS})\) is the probability of the US when the CS is present;
- \(P(\text{US}\mid\neg\text{CS})\) is the probability of the US when the CS is absent.

Suppose food occurs on 80% of tone trials but on 20% of comparable periods without the tone:

\[
\Delta P=0.80-0.20=0.60.
\]

The tone is a positive predictor of food. By contrast, if the corresponding probabilities are 0.20 and 0.80, then \(\Delta P=-0.60\): the cue predicts a lower probability of food and may acquire inhibitory significance.

This statistic is a useful summary, not a complete theory. Learning also depends on timing, stimulus salience, prior experience, and how alternative events are represented. Rescorla’s work on contingency helped establish that repeated co-occurrence alone is not sufficient to explain Pavlovian learning.

### 2.3. Acquisition, extinction, and recovery

Several recurring phenomena reveal that conditioned behavior is sensitive to context and training history:

1. **Acquisition:** a response to the CS develops during predictive training.
2. **Extinction:** responding decreases when the CS is repeatedly presented without the US.
3. **Spontaneous recovery:** an extinguished response can reappear after a delay.
4. **Renewal:** an extinguished response can return when the context changes.
5. **Reinstatement:** responding can return after exposure to the US following extinction.

These effects caution against equating extinction with complete erasure. Extinction often appears to involve learning that the cue no longer predicts the US under particular circumstances, although the balance between new learning and modification of earlier memory can vary across procedures and systems.

That distinction has practical importance. In exposure-based treatment, for example, reduced fear in one setting does not guarantee that fear will remain low in every context. The implication is not that exposure is ineffective, but that generalization, context, and return of fear matter when designing and evaluating interventions.

## 3. Operant conditioning

### 3.1. Actions and consequences

In operant conditioning, an action alters the probability of a consequence, and experience with that relation changes later behavior. A rat pressing a lever to obtain food is a standard laboratory example. In human settings, checking a phone, studying for an examination, or completing a workplace task may be influenced by consequences, although those behaviors also involve instruction, goals, and social context.

The central contingency can be expressed descriptively as:

\[
P(O\mid A,C) \neq P(O\mid \neg A,C),
\]

where \(A\) is an action, \(O\) an outcome, and \(C\) the context. This expresses that the outcome is more or less likely depending on whether the action occurs. In practice, defining comparable periods and alternative actions can be difficult.

### 3.2. Reinforcement and punishment

The technical meanings depend on the observed effect on behavior:

| Procedure | Consequence after behavior | Defining behavioral effect |
|---|---|---|
| Positive reinforcement | An event is added | Behavior increases |
| Negative reinforcement | An event is removed or avoided | Behavior increases |
| Positive punishment | An event is added | Behavior decreases |
| Negative punishment | An event is removed | Behavior decreases |

“Positive” and “negative” mean **added** and **removed**, not good and bad. Negative reinforcement is therefore not synonymous with punishment.

For example, fastening a seat belt may stop an irritating alarm. If stopping the alarm makes fastening the belt more likely in the future, the alarm’s removal has functioned as negative reinforcement. Importantly, a consequence is identified as a reinforcer by its effect under the relevant conditions, not merely by whether it appears pleasant.

### 3.3. Schedules of reinforcement

The schedule specifying when a response produces reinforcement affects response patterns:

- **Fixed ratio:** reinforcement follows a fixed number of responses.
- **Variable ratio:** the number of responses required varies around an average.
- **Fixed interval:** the first qualifying response after a fixed time is reinforced.
- **Variable interval:** the relevant interval varies around an average.

These categories are useful experimental tools, but they do not determine behavior in isolation. Response cost, deprivation, alternative rewards, timing, and the organism’s learning history also matter. Real-world claims such as “variable rewards make all users addicted” are therefore much stronger than the experimental evidence alone warrants.

## 4. Theoretical and mathematical development

### 4.1. The Rescorla–Wagner model

The Rescorla–Wagner model formalizes the idea that learning depends on the difference between what occurs and what is predicted. For cue \(i\), a common form is:

\[
\Delta V_i=\alpha_i\beta\left(\lambda-\sum_j V_j\right),
\]

where:

- \(V_i\) is the associative strength of cue \(i\);
- \(\alpha_i\) reflects the cue’s salience;
- \(\beta\) reflects learning-related properties of the outcome;
- \(\lambda\) represents the asymptotic associative strength supported by the outcome;
- \(\sum_jV_j\) is the combined associative strength of cues present.

The term in parentheses acts as a **prediction error**. If an outcome is more strongly predicted, it produces less additional learning.

#### Blocking example

Imagine two stages:

1. Cue \(A\) is paired with food until \(A\) predicts food well.
2. A compound \(A+B\) is paired with the same food.

Even though \(B\) is repeatedly paired with food, it may acquire relatively little associative strength. The established cue \(A\) already predicts the outcome, leaving little prediction error to support learning about \(B\). This is **blocking**.

The model’s strength is its parsimonious explanation of cue competition. Its limitations are also important: the basic version has no detailed representation of time within a trial, assumes relatively fixed learning-rate parameters, and does not by itself explain all forms of learning about relations among cues.

### 4.2. A simple quantitative example

For one cue, suppose:

\[
V_{t+1}=V_t+0.2(1-V_t), \qquad V_0=0.
\]

Here, \(V_t\) is modeled associative strength on trial \(t\); the outcome’s asymptotic value is set to 1; and the effective learning rate is 0.2.

The resulting values are approximately:

| Trial | \(V_t\) |
|---:|---:|
| 0 | 0.000 |
| 1 | 0.200 |
| 2 | 0.360 |
| 3 | 0.488 |
| 4 | 0.590 |
| 5 | 0.672 |
| 10 | 0.893 |

Learning is fastest early because prediction error is largest early. This is an **illustrative model**, not measured behavioral data. Associative strength is a theoretical variable, and its relationship to an observed response—such as salivation or lever pressing—requires an additional measurement assumption.

### 4.3. Reinforcement learning and temporal-difference models

Computational reinforcement learning extends conditioning analysis to sequential decisions. A simple action-value update is:

\[
Q(s_t,a_t)\leftarrow Q(s_t,a_t)
+\alpha\left[r_t+\gamma\max_a Q(s_{t+1},a)-Q(s_t,a_t)\right],
\]

where \(Q(s,a)\) estimates the value of taking action \(a\) in state \(s\), \(r_t\) is the immediate reward, \(\alpha\) is a learning rate, and \(\gamma\) discounts future value.

The bracketed expression is a temporal-difference prediction error. It compares an existing value estimate with an updated target incorporating reward and estimated future value.

This framework is powerful because it handles sequences: an action may be useful not because it produces immediate reward, but because it leads to a state from which reward is more likely. However, the equation is an algorithmic model, not a universal description of all operant behavior. Real organisms may use incomplete state representations, explicit causal knowledge, social information, or exploration strategies not captured by this simple update.

### 4.4. Goal-directed action and habit

Instrumental behavior can depend on at least two distinguishable forms of control:

- **Goal-directed control:** behavior is sensitive to the current value of the outcome and to the action–outcome contingency.
- **Habitual control:** behavior is relatively insensitive to changes in outcome value or contingency, often after extensive training.

A common test is **outcome devaluation**. An animal first learns that an action produces a particular food. The food’s value is then reduced, for instance by allowing the animal to consume it to satiety. If the action decreases when tested without the food being delivered, this supports sensitivity to outcome value.

The inference is not automatic. Researchers must verify that devaluation worked, that the animal can still perform the action, and that the test conditions do not themselves obscure knowledge. “Habit” is an experimentally defined pattern of relative insensitivity, not proof that an action is completely automatic or cognitively inaccessible.

## 5. Comparing theories critically

| Approach | Main contribution | Strength | Limitation or ambiguity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Contingency analysis | Emphasizes predictive relations between events | Moves beyond simple co-occurrence | Does not alone specify the learning mechanism |
| Rescorla–Wagner | Explains learning through prediction error and cue competition | Accounts for phenomena such as blocking | Basic form has limited timing and representational structure |
| Temporal-difference learning | Models learning across time and sequential states | Connects behavior with computational and neural hypotheses | Depends on assumptions about states, rewards, and value representation |
| Goal-directed/habit frameworks | Distinguishes outcome-sensitive from relatively insensitive action | Supports experimentally testable dissociations | Tests can be affected by motivation, performance, and design |
| Contextual and inhibitory-learning accounts | Explain recovery after extinction | Highlights context dependence and persistence of earlier learning | Mechanisms can differ across tasks and are not always uniquely identified |

No single model resolves every conditioning phenomenon. A careful comparison asks not only whether a model can fit a learning curve, but also whether it predicts new conditions, identifies the representations used by the learner, and distinguishes learning from performance.

For example, a weak observed response might reflect weak learning, but it might also reflect fatigue, competing motivation, an inability to perform, or a context that suppresses expression. These alternatives are especially consequential when a study infers mental or neural processes from one behavioral measure.

## 7. Neuroscience and multidisciplinary connections

### 7.1. Neural signals and prediction error

Research on midbrain dopamine neurons has identified activity patterns consistent with reward-prediction-error coding in many tasks. Schultz, Dayan, and Montague (1997) offered an influential account linking such signals to predictive learning.

The relationship should not be simplified to “dopamine equals pleasure” or “every dopamine signal is a prediction error.” Dopamine dynamics vary across neural circuits, timescales, behavioral states, and tasks. Mohebi and colleagues (2019), for example, reported dissociable dopamine dynamics associated with learning and motivation. Neural measurements therefore require careful alignment with computational variables and behavioral tests.

### 7.2. Clinical psychology

Conditioning contributes to theories of fear, avoidance, substance use, and exposure-based treatment. A cue associated with threat may elicit defensive responses, while avoidance can be maintained when it prevents or reduces distress.

Nevertheless, clinical difficulties cannot generally be reduced to a single conditioning history. Beliefs, developmental experiences, social conditions, biological vulnerabilities, and current stressors may all affect acquisition and persistence. Translating laboratory findings into treatment requires controlled clinical evidence, not analogy alone.

### 7.3. Education and technology

Feedback can shape learning behavior when it is timely, informative, and linked to actions students can control. Yet educational outcomes also depend on understanding, prior knowledge, autonomy, and social relationships. Rewarding an observable response is not the same as establishing conceptual mastery.

Similarly, digital platforms can use cues and intermittent consequences to influence engagement. A scientific assessment should measure actual exposure, behavior, alternatives, and individual differences rather than infer harm solely from the presence of a reinforcement schedule. Ethical design additionally requires attention to consent, transparency, and disproportionate effects on vulnerable users.

## 8. How to review a paper on conditioning

Because no paper was supplied, the following is an evaluation framework rather than a finding about a specific study.

### 8.1. Methodological quality

A strong paper should make its central contingency testable. Key questions include:

1. **Design and controls:** Does the study distinguish predictive pairing from simple exposure? Appropriate controls may include unpaired cues and outcomes, contingency degradation, or carefully defined comparison conditions.
2. **Measurement:** Is the conditioned response valid and reliable? Are acquisition and performance distinguished where possible?
3. **Sample and allocation:** Are sample size, recruitment, randomization, exclusions, and missing data reported?
4. **Timing and context:** Are cue duration, intertrial intervals, outcome timing, and environmental context specified?
5. **Analysis:** Are repeated measurements modeled appropriately? Are uncertainty intervals and effect sizes reported alongside statistical tests?
6. **Model comparison:** If computational models are used, are parameters identifiable, models compared fairly, and out-of-sample predictions or recovery analyses considered?
7. **Generalizability:** Do the authors distinguish results in a particular task, species, or sample from broader claims?

For extinction studies, investigators should also distinguish reduced responding during training from later retention, renewal, or reinstatement. For operant studies, they should establish that the action actually changed outcome probability and consider alternative explanations such as motor impairment or changes in motivation.

### 8.2. Citation consistency

Citation checking requires the manuscript and its reference list. A review should verify that:

- each empirical claim is supported by a relevant source;
- citations actually report the result attributed to them;
- theoretical models are not cited as direct evidence for untested mechanisms;
- references contain correct authors, years, titles, and publication details;
- contradictory or boundary-condition evidence is not selectively omitted.

Direct quotation should be checked against the original article, including its context and page or section. No direct article quotations are reproduced here because they cannot be verified against a supplied manuscript or source text.

### 8.3. Potential improvements for a paper

Depending on its actual design, a paper could be strengthened by preregistering primary hypotheses, reporting the full contingency structure, using complementary behavioral measures, sharing analysis code, testing model parameter recovery, and including a delayed or changed-context test. These are **conditional recommendations**, not identified defects in an unavailable paper.

## 9. Future directions and limitations

Several research questions are especially promising:

1. **Individual differences:** Which stable and situational factors explain differences in acquisition, extinction, and recovery?
2. **Learning versus expression:** Can combined behavioral, physiological, and computational measures better distinguish a changed association from suppressed performance?
3. **Ecological validity:** How well do laboratory models predict learning in complex environments with multiple cues and outcomes?
4. **Neural heterogeneity:** Which dopamine signals encode prediction errors, motivation, movement, or mixtures of these variables under different conditions?
5. **Clinical translation:** Which features of exposure training improve durable, context-generalized outcomes for different patients?
6. **Human–technology interaction:** How do learned cues and consequences interact with agency, attention, social influence, and platform design?

Methodologically, progress requires more than fitting increasingly flexible models. Researchers need designs that make competing explanations diverge, transparent reporting of uncertainty, replication, and tests of whether findings generalize beyond the original task.

## 10. Summary

Classical conditioning concerns learning predictive relations between stimuli; operant conditioning concerns how actions and consequences are related. Prediction-error models explain important phenomena such as diminishing acquisition and blocking, while reinforcement-learning models extend these ideas to sequential action. Extinction, recovery, and devaluation show why learned behavior cannot be understood solely as a fixed stimulus–response connection.

In practice, conditioning principles inform research in neuroscience, clinical psychology, education, and technology. Their application requires caution: a model’s success in a controlled task does not automatically establish a causal explanation for complex real-world behavior. A specific scientific review and citation audit would require the paper’s text and bibliography.
